<Callout variant="note" label="In short">
- Primary drying is the long, expensive phase of a lyophilisation cycle, and its recipe is usually inherited rather than designed.
- I formulated recipe design as a discrete search over piecewise-constant shelf temperature and chamber pressure schedules.
- The solver returns a schedule **and** a certificate: no schedule on the grid is faster, and the gap to the continuous optimum is bounded.
- The certificate is only as good as the steady-state model behind it. On a real product, that model is the weak link, not the search.
</Callout>

## The problem, in industrial terms

A lyophilisation cycle has three phases, and primary drying is where the time
goes. It is the phase in which ice is removed by sublimation, and on a typical
parenteral product it accounts for the majority of the cycle. A freeze-dryer is
a capital-intensive machine that is either running a batch or not earning, so
hours removed from primary drying convert directly into throughput on equipment
that has already been bought.

The lever is the recipe: a schedule of shelf temperature and chamber pressure
over the phase. In practice that schedule is very often inherited — from a
development batch, from a similar product, from a supplier's starting point —
and then frozen by validation. Nobody can say whether it is the best one,
because nobody has searched. The question I wanted to answer is narrow and
answerable: **for a given product, container and dryer, what is the fastest
admissible primary drying schedule, and how do you know?**

## Why it is hard

The obvious approach — nudge the shelf temperature up until something goes
wrong — fails for three reasons that compound.

**The feasible region is a region, not a limit.** Two constraints bound the
process from opposite directions. The product temperature at the sublimation
front must stay below the collapse temperature of the formulation, which caps
how much heat you may supply. The sublimation flux must stay below what the
dryer can remove without choked flow in the duct or overloading the condenser,
which caps how fast you may go. Raising the shelf temperature helps against one
and hurts against the other. The admissible set is the intersection, and it
moves as the cake dries.

**The system is non-stationary.** As the sublimation front recedes, the dried
layer thickens and its mass-transfer resistance grows. A setpoint pair that is
comfortably inside the design space at the start of the phase can be outside it
near the end, and the reverse is also true — which is exactly why a schedule
beats a constant.

<Sidenote summary="On the design-space framing">
The design-space construction used here — iso-lines of product temperature and
of sublimation flux plotted over shelf temperature and chamber pressure — is
standard in the freeze-drying literature. The contribution is not the map; it
is searching it exhaustively and being able to say what the search proves.
</Sidenote>

**The decisions are genuinely discrete.** A dryer does not accept a continuous
control law. It accepts a small number of segments, each with a setpoint pair
held for a duration, and its pressure control has a practical resolution. Once
the schedule is a sequence of segments drawn from a grid of admissible
setpoints, the design problem is combinatorial, and a gradient tells you
nothing about whether a better sequence exists two segments away.

## What I actually did

I built a solver that takes a product-and-equipment description and returns the
fastest admissible piecewise-constant schedule on a user-defined grid, together
with a certificate of what has been proved.

Scope, stated plainly:

- **In scope.** Primary drying only. Steady-state heat and mass balance at the
  sublimation front. Piecewise-constant schedules with a bounded number of
  segments. A single representative vial, plus a worst-case vial for the
  constraint check.
- **Out of scope.** Freezing and secondary drying. Transient thermal dynamics
  within a segment. Full batch heterogeneity beyond the two-vial treatment.
  Any claim about a specific commercial formulation.

I did not build a digital twin, and the distinction matters: this is a search
over a model that is deliberately simple enough to be exhaustively searchable.

## The formulation

For a segment held at shelf temperature $T_s$ and chamber pressure $P_c$, the
steady-state balance equates the heat reaching the sublimation front with the
enthalpy of sublimation carried away by the vapour flux:

$$
K_v A_v (T_s - T_b) = \Delta H_s \cdot \dot{m}, \qquad
\dot{m} = \frac{A_p (P_i(T_i) - P_c)}{R_p(L)}
$$

where $K_v$ is the vial heat-transfer coefficient, $T_b$ the vial bottom
temperature, $P_i$ the ice vapour pressure at the front temperature $T_i$,
$R_p(L)$ the dried-layer resistance at front position $L$, and $A_v$, $A_p$ the
heat and product areas. Solving the pair for $T_i$ and $\dot m$ at each grid
point gives the two quantities the constraints act on.

The decision variables are the segment setpoints and their switch positions,
expressed in terms of the fraction of ice removed rather than in time — which
makes the constraint checks position-based and removes the need to integrate to
find out whether a schedule is admissible.

The objective is total primary drying time, obtained by integrating the
reciprocal of the drying rate over the front position. The constraints are, at
every position within every segment:

$$
T_i(L) \le T_{\text{collapse}} - \delta, \qquad \dot m(L) \le \dot m_{\max}
$$

with $\delta$ an explicit safety margin rather than an implicit one.

<Callout variant="assumption">
Steady state within a segment; a single lumped $K_v$ per pressure; $R_p$ a
function of dried-layer thickness only; the collapse temperature known and
constant. Each of these is defensible for design-space work and each is a place
where a real product can disagree with the model.
</Callout>

## Implementation

The solver is about 2 000 lines of Python with NumPy and no solver dependency
in the default path.

The core is an exhaustive enumeration over the segment grid with two things
that make it tractable:

1. **Precomputation.** The steady-state solve is the expensive inner
   operation, and it depends only on the grid point and the front position —
   not on the schedule. So the whole $(T_s, P_c, L)$ lattice is solved once, up
   front, and stored. Enumeration afterwards is table lookup and prefix sums.
2. **Dominance pruning.** A partial schedule that reaches the same front
   position later than another, having consumed no less of every constraint
   margin, can never win. Discarding it is exact — this is what makes the
   result a certificate rather than a heuristic.

A MILP formulation of the same problem is included as a cross-check path. It is
not the default: on the grid sizes that matter, enumeration with the two
optimisations above is both faster and easier to trust, and it produces the
certificate as a by-product rather than as a claim from a black box.

<Sidenote summary="What I would rewrite">
The precomputation table is dense. It is the reason the grid resolution is
capped, and a sparse or lazily-evaluated store would lift that cap without
changing anything about the search.
</Sidenote>

## Validation

Validation happens at three levels, and it is worth being precise about which
of them has been done.

**The steady-state solve** is checked against published design-space
constructions for reference formulations: given the same inputs, the model
should reproduce the same iso-lines. This is a unit-level check and it passes
in the test suite.

**The search** is checked by brute force. On grids small enough to enumerate
without any pruning at all, the pruned search must return an identical optimum.
Any divergence is a bug in a dominance rule, and this test is the reason to
trust the certificate.

**The physical result** has not been validated against a real dryer. TODO —
this requires product temperature and Pirani/capacitance pressure data from a
run, which I do not have. Until that exists, the honest claim is that the
solver finds the optimum *of the model*, and nothing stronger.

<Callout variant="result">
The certificate is a statement about the search, not about the world. It says:
on this grid, under this model, no admissible schedule is faster, and the
distance to the continuous optimum is at most the stated bound.
</Callout>

## Limitations and next steps

- **Model risk dominates.** $R_p$ and $K_v$ carry more uncertainty than the
  optimisation removes. A schedule that is optimal under a 20 % error in $R_p$
  is not obviously better than a good schedule that is robust to it.
- **Steady state within a segment** is an approximation that degrades as
  segments get short. The solver does not currently forbid segments short
  enough for that to matter; it should.
- **A single representative vial** plus a worst case is a coarse treatment of
  batch heterogeneity. Edge vials see radiation the model handles crudely.
- **The certificate is grid-relative.** The bound on the gap to the continuous
  problem rests on a Lipschitz-type argument whose constant is estimated
  numerically. TODO — this argument needs to be written up properly; it is
  currently the least rigorous part of an otherwise rigorous claim.

Next: robust optimisation over an uncertainty set on $R_p$, so the answer is a
schedule that is good across the plausible range rather than optimal at a point
estimate. That changes the objective, not the machinery.

## What I would do differently

I built the MILP path first, because that is what one is taught to reach for,
and it was the wrong instinct. The problem is small, its structure is
dominance-friendly, and a solver would have buried the one property that makes
this project worth reading about — that the answer arrives with a proof
attached. I would start from the enumeration and add the MILP only as a
cross-check, which is the order the code is in now, arrived at backwards.
