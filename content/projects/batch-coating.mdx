<Callout variant="note" label="In short">
- A batch coating cycle on a bottleneck unit sets the length of an entire campaign.
- The physical limit everyone optimises — spray rate against drying capacity — was not what the cycle was actually spending its time on.
- A discrete-event account of the real sequence found the majority of the time in waits and repeats that were in nobody's model.
- The reduction was mostly organisational. That is the interesting finding, and it is not a modelling achievement.
</Callout>

## The problem, in industrial terms

Batch coating sits on a single unit that a whole campaign has to pass through.
Whatever the cycle takes, the campaign takes that multiplied by the number of
batches, so cycle time is campaign length is delivery date. When the unit is
the bottleneck, an hour removed from the cycle is an hour of capacity created
for free — no capital, no qualification of new equipment.

The standing assumption on the line was that the cycle was spray-rate limited:
that the coating suspension could not be applied faster because the drying air
could not remove the water fast enough without the film going tacky. That is a
real constraint, it is the one described in the literature, and it is the one
an engineer arriving on the line will try to relax first.

It was not where the time was.

## Why it is hard

**The binding constraint is not where the physics is.** The enthalpy balance on
the inlet air is a genuine ceiling, and it is easy to model. Because it is easy
to model, it gets modelled, and because it gets modelled, it gets treated as
the explanation. The steps around it — heat-up, equilibration, in-process
checks, the wait for a result, the second wait when the result requires a
decision — are not in anyone's model because they are not process engineering.
They are the majority of the clock.

**The data does not exist in usable form.** The cycle is recorded, but as batch
records written for compliance, not as a timeline. Reconstructing what actually
happened, minute by minute, across enough batches to see the distribution
rather than one anecdote, was the bulk of the work.

**Variance matters more than the mean.** A step that usually takes ten minutes
and occasionally takes ninety costs more than its average suggests, because the
schedule downstream is built around the worst case. Optimising the mean of the
wrong step is a common and expensive mistake.

<Sidenote summary="On the honesty of this project">
The satisfying version of this story is a clever model that unlocked a physical
limit. The true version is that careful measurement showed the limit was not
binding, and the saving came from removing waiting. The second version is more
useful to anyone reading it, so it is the one on this page.
</Sidenote>

## What I actually did

- **Instrumented the cycle.** Reconstructed a timeline per batch from the
  available records, to a resolution fine enough to separate the process steps
  from the waits between them, across enough batches to get a distribution.
- **Built a discrete-event model** of the sequence: operations with durations
  drawn from the measured distributions, precedence constraints, and the
  resource contentions that were actually present.
- **Modelled the physical ceiling** with an enthalpy balance on the drying air,
  so the spray-rate constraint was quantified rather than assumed.
- **Searched the sequence** — which steps can be removed, merged, run in
  parallel, or moved off the critical path — under the precedence and quality
  constraints.
- **Did not** change any formulation, any product specification, or any
  quality-relevant parameter. The saving had to come from sequencing or it did
  not count.

## The formulation

The drying ceiling. For inlet air at mass flow $\dot m_a$, the water the air
can carry away between inlet and outlet humidity sets a maximum spray rate:

$$
\dot m_{\text{spray}}^{\max} = \dot m_a \,\bigl(Y_{\text{out}}^{\max} - Y_{\text{in}}\bigr)
$$

with the outlet humidity capped below saturation by the margin the film needs
to avoid tackiness. This gives a hard lower bound on the spray phase and
nothing else — which is precisely the point: it bounds one segment of a cycle
that has many.

The sequencing problem. Operations $i$ with durations $d_i$, precedence pairs
$(i,j) \in P$, start times $s_i$, and a makespan objective:

$$
\min\; \max_i \,(s_i + d_i)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
s_j \ge s_i + d_i \;\;\forall (i,j) \in P
$$

subject to resource exclusion on the unit. Every candidate change is a
modification to $P$ or to the operation set, and each one has to be justifiable
to somebody who will have to sign the revised procedure.

<Callout variant="assumption">
Durations independent between steps; the measured distributions representative
of normal operation; quality outcomes unaffected by the sequence changes
considered. The last of these is an assumption in the model and a requirement
in reality, and it is where the burden of proof sat.
</Callout>

## Implementation

A small discrete-event simulation — a few hundred lines — with the candidate
changes enumerated rather than searched heuristically, because the operation
set is small enough that enumeration is instant and an exhaustive answer is
easier to defend in a meeting than a heuristic one.

The two things that made it useful:

1. **Sampled durations, not means.** Every run draws from the measured
   distributions, so the output is a makespan distribution and the reported
   improvement carries a spread. A point estimate would have overstated the
   result and been correctly disbelieved.
2. **Changes are tagged by who has to agree.** Each candidate carries a label:
   engineering decision, procedural change, or quality change. This turned the
   output from a list of savings into a list of conversations, ranked by hours
   saved per conversation — which is what actually got things implemented.

The structure of the result, which is the part that generalises:

- The physical spray-rate ceiling was **not** the binding constraint on the
  original cycle. It became closer to binding only after the sequencing changes.
- The majority of the removed time came from steps in the "wait" and "repeat"
  categories rather than from process operations.
- The reduction was distributed across a number of small changes, not
  concentrated in one. No single change accounted for anything like the total,
  which is why it had not been found by inspection.

## Validation

**The model was validated against the measured baseline** before any change was
proposed: fed the original sequence, it had to reproduce the observed makespan
distribution within a stated tolerance. A sequencing model that cannot
reproduce the present has no standing to predict the future.

**The drying ceiling was checked** against outlet humidity measurements at
known spray rates, which is the only way to know whether the enthalpy balance
is describing the actual air path rather than an idealised one.

**The implemented cycle was measured, not assumed.** TODO — the post-change

## Limitations and next steps

- **One product, one line.** Nothing here establishes that the same breakdown
  holds elsewhere. The method transfers; the answer does not.
- **The saving is organisational,** which means it can be lost again. A change
  in personnel or procedure can quietly reinstate a wait, and nothing in the
  model prevents that.
- **Quality was held constant by construction, not demonstrated.** The
  sequencing changes were chosen to be quality-neutral by argument; a formal
  demonstration across enough batches is a separate piece of work.
- **Distributions came from records** written for a different purpose, and
  inherit whatever rounding and habit those records contain.

Next: the same instrumentation applied before any modelling on a second line,
to test whether "the time is in the waits" is a property of this cycle or of
batch operations generally. I suspect the latter, which is exactly the kind of
suspicion that should be tested rather than published.

## What I would do differently

I spent the first weeks refining the drying model, because it was the part that
looked like process engineering. The measurement campaign that actually
answered the question was cheap and could have come first. The general lesson —
measure where the time is before modelling why it is there — is obvious in
retrospect and was not obvious to me at the time, which is the only reason it
is worth writing down.
