- Since the pharmacopoeia allows non-distillation routes, a site building WFI capacity has two compliant options rather than one.
- The two routes are usually compared on the wrong axis — capital cost — because it is the number that is easiest to obtain.
- I built a comparison whose output is a decision boundary in the uncertain parameters, not a single winner.

## The problem, in industrial terms

Water for injection is the highest-grade water a pharmaceutical site produces
and one of its larger continuous energy consumers. For decades there was
effectively one compliant way to make it: distillation, usually a
multiple-effect still. The European Pharmacopoeia's admission of
non-distillation methods opened a second route — a membrane train, typically
reverse osmosis followed by electrodeionisation and ultrafiltration, with a
sanitisation regime that carries the microbiological burden distillation
carries thermally.

So a site adding WFI capacity now has a genuine choice, and the choice is
consequential: the two routes have different capital costs, very different
energy signatures, different failure modes and different qualification effort.
In practice the decision is often made by precedent — the site already runs
stills, so it buys another still. That is not obviously wrong. It is just
undefended.

## Why it is hard

**The two routes do not fail in the same way.** A still is a thermal barrier:
it is sanitised by the fact that it boils. A membrane train is a physical
barrier maintained by a control regime, and its risk is biofilm on the
downstream side. These are not comparable on a single risk axis, and any
comparison that reduces them to one is hiding the decision rather than making
it.

**The economics depend on things the study cannot fix.** The energy comparison
turns on the price of steam relative to electricity, on the number of effects
in the still, on the plant's utilisation profile, and on whether waste heat has
somewhere to go. Every one of these is site-specific and several are volatile.
A single-point comparison is a comparison of two guesses.

**The obvious metric is the least informative one.** Capital cost is the number
that arrives first and the one everybody asks for, and over a twenty-year asset
life it is frequently not the one that decides. But life-cycle cost requires
assumptions, and assumptions get argued with, whereas a capex figure gets
believed.

<Sidenote summary="Why a boundary, not a winner">
A study that outputs "route A wins" is fragile: it is falsified by one input
moving. A study that outputs "route A wins whenever the steam-to-electricity
price ratio exceeds X, at utilisation above Y" survives the input moving, and
it tells the reader something they can check against their own site.
</Sidenote>

## What I actually did

I built a comparison framework and applied it. The framework is the deliverable
here; the application is the client's.

- **In scope.** A common functional unit and a shared system boundary; a
  life-cycle cost model with explicit annuitisation; an energy model for each
  route; a Monte Carlo sensitivity over the inputs that are genuinely
  uncertain; and a presentation of the result as a decision boundary.
- **Out of scope.** Qualification cost beyond an order-of-magnitude allowance.
  Site integration — piping runs, plant-room space, existing steam headroom.
  Monetised microbiological risk, which I judged not defensible.

The boundary definition did more work than anything else in the study. Both
routes need pretreatment, and how much of it you attribute to the WFI step
changes the answer. Fixing that boundary first, in writing, before any number
was collected, is the step I would insist on again.

## The formulation

Life-cycle cost per cubic metre of WFI, over an asset life $N$ at discount rate
$r$:

$$
C = \frac{\text{CAPEX} \cdot a(r, N) + C_{\text{energy}} + C_{\text{cons}} + C_{\text{labour}}}{V_{\text{annual}}},
\qquad a(r,N) = \frac{r(1+r)^N}{(1+r)^N - 1}
$$

The two routes differ almost entirely inside $C_{\text{energy}}$.

For a multiple-effect still with $n$ effects, the specific steam demand falls
roughly with $1/n$, so the energy term is a steam cost multiplied by a
distillate-to-steam ratio that the number of effects fixes. The still also
consumes cooling water, which is not free and is regularly left out.

For the membrane route the energy is electrical — high-pressure pumping across
the RO stages, plus the EDI current — with an added thermal term for periodic
hot-water sanitisation. That last term is small per cycle and matters because
of how often it is done, which is a control-regime decision rather than an
equipment property.

<Callout variant="assumption">
Constant demand profile; a single tariff for each energy carrier; membrane and
still both operated at design point; replacement of membranes on a fixed
schedule rather than on performance. The first and last of these are the ones
most likely to be wrong on a real site.
</Callout>

## Implementation

A parameterised model in Python, roughly 800 lines, deliberately kept small
enough that every equation in it can be traced to a line in the method note.

Two implementation choices did most of the work:

1. **Every input is a distribution, not a number.** Costs, tariffs, utilisation
   and lifetimes enter as ranges with a stated shape. A point estimate is
   available but it is a derived view, not the primary object. This makes it
   impossible to report a single number without also reporting its spread.
2. **The output is a map.** The model sweeps the two inputs the site actually
   controls or knows — the steam-to-electricity price ratio and the annual
   utilisation — and reports which route is cheaper at each point, with the
   region where the difference is smaller than the model's own uncertainty
   marked as undecided. That region is the honest part of the answer.

## Validation

**Energy figures** were checked against published specific-consumption ranges
for both technologies. Anything falling outside the literature range was
treated as an input error until proven otherwise, which caught two of them.

**The cost model** was checked by reconstruction: an independently built
spreadsheet reproducing the same case within a stated tolerance. Agreement
between two implementations of the same assumptions is weak validation, but it
does catch arithmetic, and arithmetic is what usually goes wrong.

## Limitations and next steps

- **Risk is not in the objective.** The microbiological risk profile differs
  between the routes and is the reason a conservative site chooses
  distillation regardless of cost. Leaving it out is defensible only because
  it is stated; monetising it would not have been.
- **Site integration is excluded** and can be decisive — available steam
  headroom in particular can make the still nearly free or nearly impossible.
- **A constant demand profile** flatters the still, which does not like being
  cycled.
- **Vendor data is vendor data.** Specific consumptions came from suppliers
  with an interest in the answer.

Next: a variable-demand version, because the utilisation profile is where the
two routes diverge most and it is the input a site actually knows.

## What I would do differently

I collected numbers before fixing the boundary, and paid for it by having to
re-collect a third of them. The order should have been: functional unit,
boundary, uncertainty structure, then data — and only then a conversation with
a vendor. The framework in this project is essentially that order, written down
after learning it the expensive way.
