A heuristic hands you a schedule. An exact method hands you a schedule and a
sentence: *nothing better exists in this space.* The schedules may be
identical. The sentences are not, and in an industrial setting the difference
between them is most of the value.

## What the client is actually buying

When a plant asks whether its recipe can be improved, it is not asking for a
recipe. It is asking whether to stop asking. Those are different questions and
only one of them has an end.

Give a client a better schedule and you have given them an improvement of
unknown size against an unknown ceiling. The rational response is to keep
looking — to run another study next year, to entertain the next vendor with a
better story. Give them a certified schedule and you have given them a reason
to close the file. The saving is not the eight hours you removed from the
cycle; it is the eight hours plus the studies nobody now has to commission.

<Callout variant="note">
This is why "we improved it by 12%" and "we proved 12% is the maximum" have
different values even when they describe the same schedule. The first is a
result. The second is a decision.
</Callout>

## The certificate is smaller than it sounds

It is worth being precise about what an exact method proves, because the claim
is narrower than the word "optimal" suggests and overclaiming here destroys the
trust the certificate was supposed to buy.

An exhaustive search over a discretised space proves a statement of the form:

> Under model $M$, over search space $S$, no element of $S$ has an objective
> value better than $z^\star$.

Three qualifications are load-bearing.

**Under model $M$.** If the model's transfer coefficient is 20% wrong, the
certificate is a rigorous statement about a fiction. This is the qualification
that matters most in process engineering, where the constitutive parameters are
usually the least certain thing in the room.

**Over search space $S$.** A grid is not the continuum. Proving that no grid
point is better says nothing about the points between them unless you add an
argument — a Lipschitz bound, a convexity result, a monotonicity property —
that connects them. That argument is a separate piece of work and it is
routinely skipped.

**Better than $z^\star$.** Only on the objective you wrote down. A schedule that
is time-optimal and operationally intolerable is still time-optimal.

So the honest form of the claim is not "this is the best recipe". It is "this
is the best recipe on this grid under these assumptions, and here is how far
the continuous optimum could be". That is less impressive and considerably more
useful, because it is checkable.

## Why this is more available than people assume

The reflex when a problem looks combinatorial is to reach for a metaheuristic,
and it is often the wrong reflex at the scale process design actually operates
at.

Many design problems have small decision spaces. A handful of segments, a
handful of setpoints per segment, a resolution set by what the equipment can
physically hold. That is thousands or millions of candidates, not $10^{30}$.
With a dominance rule — a partial solution that is behind on the objective and
no better on every constraint margin can be discarded exactly — enumeration
finishes in the time it takes to make coffee, and it finishes with a proof.

<Sidenote summary="Where this stops working">
Scheduling problems with many resources and long horizons blow past this
quickly, and there the metaheuristic is the right tool. The point is not that
exact methods always win; it is that the question "is this small enough to
enumerate?" is worth asking before assuming it is not.
</Sidenote>

The cost of the exact route is usually not compute. It is the discipline of
writing the model down completely enough to enumerate it — every constraint
explicit, every assumption named, no tuning parameters smuggled in as
"engineering judgement". That discipline is the actual deliverable, and a
heuristic lets you avoid it.

## What it changes about how you write

If you are going to hand over a certificate, the model has to be legible. Not
merely correct — legible, so that the person deciding whether to trust it can
find the assumption they doubt and check it.

In practice that means the assumption list is part of the result rather than an
appendix, the safety margins are explicit numbers rather than conservative
choices buried in a coefficient, and the failure of the certificate — the case
where the model is wrong — is described before anyone asks.

None of this is specific to optimisation. It is what makes a piece of
engineering reviewable rather than merely deliverable. Optimality certificates
just make it unavoidable, because a proof about an illegible model is worth
nothing and everyone can see that.
